# The chapters modules: three lessons on the open simulation

*For teachers, conservation partners and readers of the research who have not seen the code.
A file or a setting is named in backticks only where someone might go and look.*

Avian Age is a game about keeping moa alive on the mountain tops of glacial Aotearoa. The player
is a kaitiaki, a guardian, with a few tools: storms that hide a moa from the eagle, food plants
to grow, a waterhole. Everything else, the moa, the eagle, the kea and the forest birds, lives by
its own rules in a running ecosystem.

The game has two ways of teaching those rules.

- **Stories** are the original lessons. A script decides when the eagle attacks, walks the
  family along a path and ticks the goals. They teach clearly, and they stay in the game
  unchanged, under their own heading in the menu.
- **Chapters** are the same three lessons rebuilt the other way round. The ecosystem runs
  freely from the first second. The lesson only sets the stage, measures what happened and
  speaks about it. Nothing in it moves an animal.

The second way is slower to teach and harder to predict, and it is the one this document is
about. The bet is that a learner who works out what to do from what they can see (where the
moa are drifting, how hungry the eagle looks, that the pātōtara has no berries in winter) has
learned the rule, where one who follows a prompt has learned the prompt. The two sets exist
side by side so that this can be tested.

---

## How a chapters lesson works

**The stage is set, then left alone.** When a lesson starts, a few things are placed by hand: a
nest, two parents beside it, an egg, a standing storm or two, the waterhole in the valley. From
then on the animals run on the open simulation's rules. The parents get hungry and go to feed.
The egg hatches on its own clock. The eagle hunts when its hunger says so, whatever it can see.

**The lesson watches and speaks.** Each season is a chapter with goals that measure what the
ecosystem did (did the chick hatch, is the family at the water) and prompts that answer its
events (an eagle has locked on: "Move a storm over her"). Prompts name the tool and the target.
Explanations come after the player has acted, not instead of acting.

**One thing per lesson is guaranteed.** A lesson usually needs one event to happen so it can be
taught: the first hunt, the first kea at the nest. The lesson makes that event likely first (it
makes the eagle hungry and brings it over the family), and only if it still has not happened by
a deadline does it force it. The game records which way it went. Across many runs that record is
the measure of whether the stage was set well.

**Two sketches of every lesson.** Each lesson comes in a Guided and an Open sketch, as small
buttons under one card in the menu. Guided guarantees the first demonstration, waits for the
chick to hatch before the season turns, gives the eagle a long wind-up and pauses play on the
first prompt of each kind. Open guarantees nothing, has a shorter wind-up, never pauses, and may
have a second eagle. The lesson is the same; only the dial is different. More sketches can be
added in a few lines, and a study can compare them on the same seeds.

**The eagle can be read.** The Pouākai is a creature with needs, not a timer. Its hunger bar is
always visible; it pulses amber as the bird nears hunting; every hunt opens with a wind-up in
which the eagle shows a "!" and cannot yet strike; and a tap on it says in words how hungry it
is and what it is doing. A player who hides one moa should expect the eagle to turn to other
prey, and by preference to a species with numbers to spare.

**Problems have more than one answer.** A hungry family can be fed by sprouting pātōtara in the
warm months, by growing wharariki in winter, or by tapping wild plants to lead them to food. A
hunted moa can be covered, or the eagle can be left to take a herd animal instead. Deaths are
honest: the loss text says whether the eagle took a chick or it starved, and what would have
helped.

## The Storm (Āwhā)

**The rule it teaches.** A moa under a storm cannot be seen by the Pouākai.

**What the player sees.** A summer on the tops with one upland moa family: Mama, Papa and an
egg at the nest, two standing storms nearby, and a herd of stout-legged moa on the low ground.
The eagle grows hungry and locks onto one of the parents; the prompt is to move a storm over
her. With her hidden, the eagle turns to the herd, and a line explains why: it has to eat, and
the herd are many. The egg hatches and the chick is Pukepuke. In autumn the family drifts
downhill towards the tarn, storms blow out and have to be called again, and the Storm tool
unlocks when the first one goes. Winter is by the water with a hungrier eagle. In spring the
family must be home at the nest.

**What runs on its own.** The parents' feeding and their drift downhill and back up (their
preferred height follows the season). The egg's clock. The eagle's hunger, its choice of prey,
every hunt and every catch. Who, if anyone, is taken.

**What is guaranteed.** Only the first hunt, and only in the Guided sketch: if the eagle has not
locked onto a family member by thirty seconds in, it is pointed at one. In the Open sketch the
hunt is invited and left to happen. In stand-in play it came naturally in every run of both
sketches (see the numbers below).

**The two sketches.** Guided: one eagle, four standing storms, a six-second wind-up, the summer
waits for the hatch, prompts pause play the first time, losing any parent ends the lesson.
Open: the eagle may breed to two, two storms, a three-and-a-half-second wind-up, no waiting, no
pauses, only Pukepuke's loss ends it.

## Pātōtara

**The rule it teaches.** Sprouted pātōtara berries draw kea, and moa gathered at a nest make a
raid fail.

**What the player sees.** Two families now: Mama, Papa and the half-grown Pukepuke at their
nest, and the neighbours with an egg of their own. A bare pātōtara patch stands by the
neighbours' nest; the prompt is to press and hold it to sprout it. Berries draw kea from the
forest below. A kea that reaches the egg rolls the odds, which fall with every moa standing
near, so the way to defend the nest is to keep the families fed beside it. The egg that
survives hatches as Koukou. Autumn and winter follow as before, with one more thing to notice:
pātōtara bears nothing in winter, so the family by the water needs wharariki instead.

**What runs on its own.** The kea's interest in berries and in the egg, the raid's odds, the
families' feeding, the eagle.

**What is guaranteed.** The arrival of kea at the sprouted patch, in the Guided sketch only: two
more kea are set down near it if none has come within forty-five seconds of the sprouting. The
raid itself is rolled at real odds in both sketches. In the Guided sketch the odds reach zero
with enough moa near; in the Open sketch they never fall below one in twenty. A lost egg is not
a reset: the neighbours lay again, and the lesson goes on.

**The two sketches.** Guided: three kea, a steeper penalty per defending moa, the first prompts
pause. Open: four kea, a gentler penalty, a floor on the raid's odds, no pauses.

## The five years (Pukepuke & Koukou)

**The rule it teaches.** Every creature breeds in its own season, and a hungry Pouākai takes
whatever it can see.

**What the player sees.** Five years on the same tops with the whole cast. Two families each
start with an egg; the chicks are Pukepuke and Koukou, and the one task is to keep those two
alive until the fifth spring turns. Around them live kea, kākā and kōkako in the forest, giant
moa on the flats, and one eagle. Each year has the same shape: summer at the nests, autumn down
to the water, winter by it, spring back up. The tools are the three already learned. Prompts
come only when something has been wanted for a while. The first chick of each kind is shown as
a moment: kea in late winter, kākā and kōkako in spring, a giant moa chick in summer.

**What runs on its own.** Everything. Every species lays in its own season by a calendar (the
giant moa one egg every two or three years, and its young take eight years to grow). The eagle
hunts birds as well as moa when hungry enough, prefers the species with the most to spare, and
rests after a meal. The families breed; by year three there are wild upland moa on the tops that
nobody named.

**What is guaranteed.** Nothing beyond the stage at the start. The Guided sketch keeps a floor of
two under each of the other species, so the eagle always has a herd to turn to; the Open sketch
has no floors, and the eagle may breed.

## Choosing a sketch

| | Guided | Open |
|---|---|---|
| First demonstration | Guaranteed by a deadline | Invited, then left to the ecosystem |
| The season clock | Waits for the lesson's key goal (with a time limit) | Never waits |
| Eagle wind-up | Six seconds | Three and a half seconds |
| Prompts | Pause play the first time | Never pause |
| Eagles | One | May breed to two |
| Herd protection | A floor of two on the other species | None |
| Suits | A first session, a younger class, a demonstration on a projector | A second play, confident players, the research condition nearest to open play |

Both sketches are built for ages eleven to fourteen: short lines, one idea each, te reo names
beside the English, prompts that name the tool and the target, at most three panels before
play begins.

## What the game records

Every run can be replayed exactly from a **seed** (a number in the address bar), so two
researchers can watch the same five years. In a recorded run the game writes two files: a table
of populations every ten seconds, with the seed, the lesson, the sketch and the chapter on every
row and the story's events in the last column; and a log of the story itself: every chapter,
goal, prompt, and each guaranteed beat marked **natural** or **forced**. The share of natural
beats across many seeds is the number that says how emergent a sketch really is.

A command-line tool plays a lesson headless across any number of seeds with a stand-in player
(one who covers a hunted family member with a storm, sprouts bare pātōtara and grows wharariki
in winter, and nothing else) and prints one line per run. Its results from this build, three
seeds per sketch unless noted:

| Lesson, sketch | Won | First beat natural | Eagle took | Named birds lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Storm, Guided | 2 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 8 stout-legged, 4 upland | none |
| Storm, Open | 3 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 15 stout-legged, 5 upland | none |
| Pātōtara, Guided | 2 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 3 stout-legged, 5 upland | Koukou starved by the water in one run |
| Pātōtara, Open | 3 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 6 stout-legged, 5 upland | a neighbour taken in one run |
| Five years, Guided | 3 of 3 | (no beats) | 10 giant moa, 22 upland, 2 kea, 2 kōkako | none |
| Five years, Open (2 seeds) | 2 of 2 | (no beats) | 9 giant moa, 41 upland, 3 kea, 1 kōkako | none |

The stand-in is not a player: it never leads a family to the water or home, so its losses are
the lessons' tasks left undone (a family not home by spring; a chick hungry by the water), and
its wins say only that a lesson can be won by doing the one thing it teaches. The eagle's
catches show the surplus rule at work: in the first two lessons it fed mostly on the herd; in the
five years, on the giant moa and on the families' many wild descendants. Keep the files from a
batch; this build's are in `tests/chapters/`.

## Making a lesson for a partner's brief

A new lesson starts from a copy of the template file (`levels/_template_chapters.js`) and
needs five decisions: the one rule it teaches, written as a single line (the **claim**); the
Field Guide entries it rests on; the land and who is on stage at the start; the one event that
must be demonstrated, and how it is invited before it is forced; and the prompts that answer
what the ecosystem does. The words all live in the lesson file, so a partner can read and
change them without touching a rule.

A checker runs over a lesson without playing it and refuses the common mistakes: a lesson with
no claim, a prompt that waits on an event nothing fires, a beat that forces an attack outside
its guarantee, a season held with no time limit. The batch tool then plays it across seeds to
show whether the first demonstration comes on its own. Both are described in
`MODULE_SCRIPTING.md`, which lists every key the format has.

## Known limits and notes for tuning

- The stand-in's losses in the first two lessons are winter hunger by the water, the one task
  it does not attempt. A real player has the tools; whether a class finds them unprompted is
  the question to watch.
- Moa get hungry at about six tenths of the open simulation's rate in these lessons, because
  the land is small. The setting is one line per lesson and may want retuning after play.
- In the five-year run that was checked, kea laid every winter and kākā once in five springs,
  as their calendars allow, but kōkako laid only once in five springs although their calendar
  gives them four chances in five. Worth a look before the free play is used for anything that
  counts bird numbers.
- Nothing yet lets a lesson order a named bird to walk somewhere and hand it back, and there is
  no tension curve on the eagle's hunger over a season; both are planned. Until then the
  deadline on a beat is the only guarantee.
- Each lesson opens on fresh land. The hand-off that walks a family from one lesson's land onto
  the next (as the Stories do) is not built for chapters.
- The Field Guide shows every entry from the start; the entries a lesson names are not yet
  revealed as the lesson goes.
