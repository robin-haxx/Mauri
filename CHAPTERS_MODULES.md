# The chapters modules: three lessons on the open simulation

*For teachers, conservation partners and readers of the research who have not seen the code.
A file or a setting is named in backticks only where someone might go and look.*

Avian Age is a game about keeping moa alive on the mountain tops of glacial Aotearoa. The player
is a kaitiaki, a guardian, with a few tools: storms that hide a moa from the eagle, food plants
to grow, a waterhole. Everything else, the moa, the eagle, the kea and the forest birds, lives by
its own rules in a running ecosystem.

The game has two ways of teaching those rules.

- **Stories** are the original lessons. A script decides when the eagle attacks, walks the
  family along a path and ticks the goals. They teach clearly, and they stay in the game
  unchanged, under their own heading in the menu.
- **Chapters** are the same three lessons rebuilt the other way round. The ecosystem runs
  freely from the first second. The lesson only sets the stage, measures what happened and
  speaks about it. Nothing in it moves an animal.

The second way is slower to teach and harder to predict, and it is the one this document is
about. The bet is that a learner who works out what to do from what they can see (where the
moa are drifting, how hungry the eagle looks, that the pātōtara has no berries in winter) has
learned the rule, where one who follows a prompt has learned the prompt. The two sets exist
side by side so that this can be tested.

---

## How a chapters lesson works

**The stage is set, then left alone.** When a lesson starts, a few things are placed by hand: a
nest, two parents beside it, an egg, a standing storm or two, the waterhole in the valley. From
then on the animals run on the open simulation's rules. The parents get hungry and go to feed.
The egg hatches on its own clock. The eagle hunts when its hunger says so, whatever it can see.

**The lesson watches and speaks.** Each season is a chapter with goals that measure what the
ecosystem did (did the chick hatch, is the family at the water) and prompts that answer its
events (an eagle has locked on: "Move a storm over her"). Prompts name the tool and the target.
Explanations come after the player has acted, not instead of acting.

**One thing per lesson is guaranteed.** A lesson usually needs one event to happen so it can be
taught: the first hunt, the first kea at the nest. The lesson makes that event likely first (it
makes the eagle hungry and brings it over the family), and only if it still has not happened by
a deadline does it force it. The game records which way it went. Across many runs that record is
the measure of whether the stage was set well.

**Three sketches of every lesson.** Each lesson comes as small buttons under one card in the
menu: Guided, Open and Clues. The lesson is the same in all three; what differs is how much is
guaranteed and how much is said. Guided guarantees the first demonstration, waits for the chick
to hatch before the season turns, gives the eagle a long wind-up and tells the player what to
do the moment a problem appears. Open guarantees nothing, has a shorter wind-up and never
pauses. Clues keeps Guided's guarantees and turns the teaching round: it says nothing when a
problem appears, asks a question if the player has not acted, and only then says what to do.
More sketches can be added in a few lines, and a study can compare them on the same seeds.

**The eagle can be read.** The Pouākai is a creature with needs, not a timer. Its hunger bar is
always visible; it pulses amber as the bird nears hunting; every hunt opens with a wind-up in
which the eagle shows a "!" and cannot yet strike; and a tap on it says in words how hungry it
is and what it is doing. A player who hides one moa should expect the eagle to turn to other
prey, and by preference to a species with numbers to spare.

**Problems have more than one answer.** A hungry family can be fed by sprouting pātōtara in the
warm months, by growing wharariki in winter, or by tapping wild plants to lead them to food. A
hunted moa can be covered, or the eagle can be left to take a herd animal instead. Deaths are
honest: the loss text says whether the eagle took a chick or it starved, and what would have
helped.

## The Storm (Āwhā)

**The rule it teaches.** A moa under a storm cannot be seen by the Pouākai.

**What the player sees.** A summer on the tops with one upland moa family: Mama, Papa and an
egg at the nest, two standing storms nearby, and a herd of stout-legged moa on the low ground.
The eagle grows hungry and locks onto one of the parents; the prompt is to move a storm over
her. With her hidden, the eagle turns to the herd, and a line explains why: it has to eat, and
the herd are many. The egg hatches and the chick is Pukepuke. In autumn the family drifts
downhill towards the tarn, storms blow out and have to be called again, and the Storm tool
unlocks when the first one goes. Winter is by the water with a hungrier eagle. In spring the
family must be home at the nest.

**What runs on its own.** The parents' feeding and their drift downhill and back up (their
preferred height follows the season). The egg's clock. The eagle's hunger, its choice of prey,
every hunt and every catch. Who, if anyone, is taken.

**What is guaranteed.** Only the first hunt, and only in the Guided sketch: if the eagle has not
locked onto a family member by thirty seconds in, it is pointed at one. In the Open sketch the
hunt is invited and left to happen. In stand-in play it came naturally in every run of both
sketches (see the numbers below).

**The two sketches.** Guided: one eagle, four standing storms, a six-second wind-up, the summer
waits for the hatch, prompts pause play the first time, losing any parent ends the lesson.
Open: the eagle may breed to two, two storms, a three-and-a-half-second wind-up, no waiting, no
pauses, only Pukepuke's loss ends it.

## Pātōtara

**The rule it teaches.** Sprouted pātōtara berries draw kea, and moa gathered at a nest make a
raid fail.

**What the player sees.** Two families now: Mama, Papa and the half-grown Pukepuke at their
nest, and the neighbours with an egg of their own. A bare pātōtara patch stands by the
neighbours' nest; the prompt is to press and hold it to sprout it. Berries draw kea from the
forest below. A kea that reaches the egg rolls the odds, which fall with every moa standing
near, so the way to defend the nest is to keep the families fed beside it. The egg that
survives hatches as Koukou. Autumn and winter follow as before, with one more thing to notice:
pātōtara bears nothing in winter, so the family by the water needs wharariki instead.

**What runs on its own.** The kea's interest in berries and in the egg, the raid's odds, the
families' feeding, the eagle.

**What is guaranteed.** The arrival of kea at the sprouted patch, in the Guided sketch only: two
more kea are set down near it if none has come within forty-five seconds of the sprouting. The
raid itself is rolled at real odds in both sketches. In the Guided sketch the odds reach zero
with enough moa near; in the Open sketch they never fall below one in twenty. A lost egg is not
a reset: the neighbours lay again, and the lesson goes on.

**The two sketches.** Guided: three kea, a steeper penalty per defending moa, the first prompts
pause. Open: four kea, a gentler penalty, a floor on the raid's odds, no pauses.

## The five years (Pukepuke & Koukou)

**The rule it teaches.** Every creature breeds in its own season, and a hungry Pouākai takes
whatever it can see.

**What the player sees.** Five years on the same tops with the whole cast. Two families each
start with an egg; the chicks are Pukepuke and Koukou, and the one task is to keep those two
alive until the fifth spring turns. Around them live kea, kākā and kōkako in the forest, giant
moa on the flats, and one eagle. Each year has the same shape: summer at the nests, autumn down
to the water, winter by it, spring back up. The tools are the three already learned. Prompts
come only when something has been wanted for a while. The first chick of each kind is shown as
a moment: kea in late winter, kākā and kōkako in spring, a giant moa chick in summer.

**What runs on its own.** Everything. Every species lays in its own season by a calendar (the
giant moa one egg every two or three years, and its young take eight years to grow). The eagle
hunts birds as well as moa when hungry enough, prefers the species with the most to spare, and
rests after a meal. The families breed; by year three there are wild upland moa on the tops that
nobody named.

**What is guaranteed.** Nothing beyond the stage at the start. The Guided sketch keeps a floor of
two under each of the other species, so the eagle always has a herd to turn to; the Open sketch
has no floors, and the eagle may breed.

## The endless game on the lessons' rules (The Tops)

**What it is.** The endless game the three lessons lead to, as a new card in the menu's Endless
section beside the first Endless level, which is unchanged. The two families from the lessons
live on one stretch of country, with a herd on the flats, bush moa and birds in the forest and
one Pouākai. The years go round without end, and the same animals live through them: the chick
that hatches in year one is half grown in year two and a parent in year four.

**Why it is a different game from the first Endless level.** That level sets down a fresh cast
in a new area every year and asks for herds of eight or ten. This one is built so the player
knows each animal.

- **Small herds that grow slowly.** Every moa keeps a breeding calendar: one egg in its season,
  a year or two between clutches, three years to grow up. In stand-in play the upland moa went
  from four to about ten over six years.
- **A chick stays with its mother** for its first two years, and is visibly small.
- **Tended food decides breeding and winters.** Wild browse only partly feeds the upland
  families. Hens that are fed on the pātōtara and wharariki the player grows lay; hens that are
  not, mostly do not. Winter falls hardest on each year's chicks.
- **A hunt is an event.** The eagle has a slow appetite and hunts about once a season. Every
  hunt opens with a six-second wind-up on the bird it has picked, and it never strikes a bird it
  has not wound up on. Hide its bird twice and it gives the family up for a while and goes
  after a herd with numbers to spare.
- **Nothing is protected from dying out.** When a herd falls low, a few arrive over the pass a
  season or two later. A lost eagle is replaced the same way.
- **The clan stays small.** Once there are more than ten upland moa, the oldest grown young walk
  off over the pass each spring to found families elsewhere. Sending them is the long game:
  the count of them is the score that keeps rising.
- **The player defends nests and does not raid them.** Kea raid at odds the gathered moa set,
  as the Pātōtara lesson taught. From year two a Berry Cache draws kea away from the nests,
  which is the same rule used from the other side.

**The year.** Summer at the nests (eggs, kea, the first hunts). Autumn (get wharariki growing).
Winter (keep every upland moa alive). Spring (the year's count, and the grown young leave).
Kākā arrive in year two. From year three two speargrass patches found a new nest. A second
eagle may come from year five if the land holds many moa. The cold deepens slowly over about
fourteen years.

**What carries over from the first Endless level:** its country and biomes, the deepening
climate, the Berry Cache, nest founding with a favoured plant, the birds. **What is reworked:**
breeding, goals, income, the eagle, protection. **What is left out of this first version:** the
tour of a 2×2 world, the mast year and the kākāpō, the player's Nest Raid, and the forest tools.

| Slower: reacts in 5 s, feeds | 0 of 6 | 12 to 14 | 3 to 8 | 0 | 845 to 1165 | 269 to 635 |
| Late: reacts in 8 s, feeds | 0 of 6 | 10 to 13 | 1 to 7 | 0 to 2 | 805 to 1270 | 81 to 549 |

named bird or two over nine years.

### The player's camera

On this level the player can look closer. Two buttons under the pause controls zoom in and out
in four steps, and they are finger-sized for a touchscreen. The mouse wheel zooms on a desktop,
and the + and - keys work too. A drag on the land pans when zoomed in. A tap on any animal rings
it, says who it is (its name if it has one, its kind, sex and age, how well fed it is, what it
is doing, whose young it is) and makes the view follow it; a second tap, or the cross on the
"Following" label, lets it go. When the view is zoomed in elsewhere and an eagle starts on an
upland moa, the view eases over to frame hunter and hunted, and eases back when the hunt is
over. All of it is drawn-view only and costs nothing the simulation can feel. Any level can
switch it on with one line (`userCamera: true`); pinch-to-zoom is not built.

## The Clues sketch: teaching by clue first

The Guided and Open sketches put the answer on screen the moment the eagle locks on: "Move a
STORM over it". The Clues sketch is built to find out whether a player has learned the rule
or only followed the prompt.

**The hint ladder.** When the simulation raises a problem, nothing is said at first. What the
player can see is the clue: the eagle's "!" over the bird it has picked, its amber hunger bar,
a storm thinning. If the problem is still there a few seconds on, a question is asked ("The
Pouākai has seen Mama Moa. It can only take what it can see. What could hide Mama Moa?"). Only
after that is the player told what to do. The first hunt of a lesson climbs the ladder quickly
and its last rung stops play, so nobody loses a bird to not knowing the rule; later hunts climb
it slowly. The eagle's wind-up is eight seconds in this sketch, to leave room for the ladder.
The same ladder is used for a storm blowing out and for kea circling a nest.

**A word for getting there first.** A player who hides the bird before any hint is told so, in
words that state the rule: "Hidden in time, with no hint. The Pouākai can't take what it can't
see."

**One more season with nothing said.** After spring, each lesson has one more summer in which
no hint is given at all. In the Pātōtara lesson the neighbours have a new egg. How the player
does there is the test of the lesson. The win card says "Without a hint: 3 of 5. On your own at
the end: 2 of 2."

**Honest ecology.** No species is protected by a floor: when the herd runs low a few arrive over
the pass, and the lesson says so. Hide its bird twice and the eagle gives the family up and goes
for the herd. The families are on the breeding calendar, so a chick is small, stays beside its
mother, and in the five years grows up over years, which makes that lesson's claim true of
the birds the player is watching.

**What the record gains.** Each problem is logged with the rung at which it was answered, how
long it took and how it ended, and every storm moved, patch sprouted, plant tapped or animal
tapped is logged with its second. That is the measure of the learner the earlier record lacked.

### Where your judgement is wanted in the Clues sketches

- **How long before each hint.** The first hunt asks its question at two seconds and gives the
  instruction at five; later hunts at four and seven. The wind-up is eight seconds. These are
  one line in `levels/_chapters_kit.js`. Too short and it is Guided again; too long and a slow
  player loses a bird before being told.
- **Whether the opening should still state the rule.** The lessons' opening lines are the same
  in all three sketches, so the Storm lesson still says "a moa under a storm can't be seen"
  before play begins. As built, Clues tests whether the player *applies* a rule they were told.
  Dropping that line would test whether they *discover* it, which is harder and closer to your
  first design rule.
- **Leading the family to the water and home is not required** in this sketch, where it loses
  the level in Guided. It is still a goal with a reward.
- **The unprompted summer.** The standing storms thin out in fifteen seconds so the family is
  in the open when the eagle comes. One summer may be too short to see more than one hunt.
- **Which sketch a lesson opens on.** The menu card still opens Guided; Clues is its third
  button.

## Choosing a sketch

| | Guided | Open | Clues |
|---|---|---|---|
| First demonstration | Guaranteed by a deadline | Invited, then left to the ecosystem | Guaranteed by a deadline |
| When a problem appears | The instruction, at once | The instruction, at once, without pausing | Nothing; then a question; then the instruction |
| The season clock | Waits for the lesson's key goal (with a time limit) | Never waits | Waits, as Guided |
| Eagle wind-up | Six seconds | Three and a half seconds | Eight seconds |
| Herd protection | A floor of two | A floor of two (none in the five years) | None; newcomers over the pass |
| The family's breeding | Any season | Any season | By the calendar; chicks grow over years |
| A stretch with nothing said | No | No | One more summer |
| The record shows | How the stage behaved | How the stage behaved | Also how each problem was answered |
| Suits | A first session, a younger class, a projector | Confident players | Finding out what was learned; the research condition |

All three are built for ages eleven to fourteen: short lines, one idea each, te reo names
beside the English, at most three panels before play begins.

## What the game records

A **seed** (a number in the address bar) fixes the land and the opening stage on any computer.
On one computer and browser it also replays the whole run exactly. Across computers it does not
yet: the same seed starts the same and drifts apart within about half a minute of play
(`ENGINE_ASSESSMENT.md` §16.4), so a replay for research has to be made on the machine that made
the original. In a recorded run the game writes two files: a table
of populations every ten seconds, with the seed, the lesson, the sketch and the chapter on every
row and the story's events in the last column; and a log of the story itself: every chapter,
goal, prompt, and each guaranteed beat marked **natural** or **forced**. The share of natural
beats across many seeds is the number that says how emergent a sketch really is.

A command-line tool plays a lesson headless across any number of seeds with a stand-in player
(one who covers a hunted family member with a storm, sprouts bare pātōtara and grows wharariki
in winter, and nothing else) and prints one line per run. Its results from this build, three
seeds per sketch unless noted:

| Lesson, sketch | Won | First beat natural | Eagle took | Named birds lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Storm, Guided | 2 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 8 stout-legged, 4 upland | none |
| Storm, Open | 3 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 15 stout-legged, 5 upland | none |
| Pātōtara, Guided | 2 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 3 stout-legged, 5 upland | Koukou starved by the water in one run |
| Pātōtara, Open | 3 of 3 | 3 of 3 | 6 stout-legged, 5 upland | a neighbour taken in one run |
| Five years, Guided | 3 of 3 | (no beats) | 10 giant moa, 22 upland, 2 kea, 2 kōkako | none |
| Five years, Open (2 seeds) | 2 of 2 | (no beats) | 9 giant moa, 41 upland, 3 kea, 1 kōkako | none |

The stand-in is not a player: it never leads a family to the water or home, so its losses are
the lessons' tasks left undone (a family not home by spring; a chick hungry by the water), and
its wins say only that a lesson can be won by doing the one thing it teaches. The eagle's
catches show the surplus rule at work: in the first two lessons it fed mostly on the herd; in the
five years, on the giant moa and on the families' many wild descendants.

**Read the win counts with care.** Three seeds are too few, and the counts moved when nine of
these runs were repeated on another machine (seven wins in the build container, five there).
What held in all eighteen runs: the first demonstration came on its own, no named bird was taken
by the eagle, and every named bird that died had starved. The files in `tests/chapters/` for
both Storm sketches and the Guided Pātōtara sketch are from that second machine (5 November),
so they differ from the table above; the rest are from the build container.

## What the lessons do not teach yet

Each lesson lists the rules of the simulation it teaches. A tool (`node tools/coverage.js`)
reads every level, works out which rules it plays by, and reports the ones no lesson teaches.
This replaces the hand-kept list, and it is the map for the lessons still to be written.

| Level | Rules in force | Taught by a lesson | Untaught | Plays against a taught rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Endless: The Tops (the new variant) | 16 | 12 | 4 | 0 |
| Endless: Mutunga kore (the first Endless level) | 16 | 5 | 9 | 2 |

**Untaught in the new variant:** that wild browse only partly feeds the families, the
waterhole, where mauri comes from, and the deepening cold.

**Untaught in the first Endless level:** the Nest Raid, the waterhole, the fern shelter, where
mauri comes from, the yearly focus species, the deepening cold, the mast year, the forest tools
and the world tour. **It also plays against two taught rules:** its storm stuns the eagle where
the lessons teach that a storm hides a moa, and it protects species with floors where the Clues
sketches teach that newcomers come over the pass.

When a new mechanic is built, add it to the table at the top of the tool, and add its name to
the `teaches` list of the lesson that covers it.

## Checking replays between two machines

A seed replays a run exactly on one machine and browser, and not yet across machines. To find
out why, run this on your own machine and compare the file it writes with the one recorded on
the build container, `tests/replay/storms_guided_seed1_build_container.txt`:

```bash
node tools/replaydiff.js --level chapters_storms --sketch Guided --seed 1 --seconds 60 --out mine.txt
```

```bash
diff mine.txt tests/replay/storms_guided_seed1_build_container.txt | head
```

The first line of each file is a fingerprint of the browser's own arithmetic, function by
function. If that line differs, the browser's math is the cause and the line names the
functions. If it matches and a later line differs, the cause is elsewhere and the line gives
the second the runs part.

## Making a lesson for a partner's brief

A new lesson starts from a copy of the template file (`levels/_template_chapters.js`) and
needs five decisions: the one rule it teaches, written as a single line (the **claim**); the
Field Guide entries it rests on; the land and who is on stage at the start; the one event that
must be demonstrated, and how it is invited before it is forced; and the prompts that answer
what the ecosystem does. The words all live in the lesson file, so a partner can read and
change them without touching a rule.

A checker runs over a lesson without playing it and refuses the common mistakes: a lesson with
no claim, a prompt that waits on an event nothing fires, a beat that forces an attack outside
its guarantee, a season held with no time limit. The batch tool then plays it across seeds to
show whether the first demonstration comes on its own. Both are described in
`MODULE_SCRIPTING.md`, which lists every key the format has.

## Known limits and notes for tuning

- The stand-in's losses in the first two lessons are winter hunger by the water, the one task
  it does not attempt. A real player has the tools; whether a class finds them unprompted is
  the question to watch.
- Moa get hungry at about six tenths of the open simulation's rate in these lessons, because
  the land is small. The setting is one line per lesson and may want retuning after play.
- In the five-year run that was checked, kea laid every winter and kākā once in five springs,
  as their calendars allow, but kōkako laid only once in five springs although their calendar
  gives them four chances in five. Worth a look before the free play is used for anything that
  counts bird numbers.
- Nothing yet lets a lesson order a named bird to walk somewhere and hand it back, and there is
  no tension curve on the eagle's hunger over a season; both are planned. Until then the
  deadline on a beat is the only guarantee.
- Each lesson opens on fresh land. The hand-off that walks a family from one lesson's land onto
  the next (as the Stories do) is not built for chapters.
- The Field Guide shows every entry from the start; the entries a lesson names are not yet
  revealed as the lesson goes.
